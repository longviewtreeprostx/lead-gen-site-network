Signs a tree needs to come down

Not every problem tree is obvious. Some of the most dangerous ones look fine from the street. Here's what actually signals a removal, rather than a trim, is the right call:

Root and base damage

Mushrooms or fungal growth (conks) at the base or on exposed roots usually mean internal rot, even if the tree still has leaves. Soil heaving or lifting on one side of the trunk is often a sign the root system has already partially failed.

Lean and structural cracks

A new or increasing lean, especially after wind or heavy rain, means the root plate may be compromised. Vertical cracks in the trunk, or a deep split where two major limbs meet (included bark), are both structural failure points that don't heal — they get worse.

Canopy dieback

Dead branches scattered through the upper canopy, especially on a tree that used to be full, often point to root stress, disease, or drought damage that's more serious than what's visible from the ground.

If you're not sure whether what you're seeing calls for removal or just trimming, that's exactly what a free on-site look is for — we'll tell you honestly which one it is, including when the answer is "it's fine, just keep an eye on it."

Dead Tree Removal

A dead tree is not a "wait and see" situation — dead wood is unpredictable and can fail without warning, especially in wind or after a hard rain. If a tree on your property has stopped leafing out, has significant dead limbs, or shows fungus/rot at the base, it's worth a look before storm season, not after a limb comes down.

Large Tree Removal

Big trees need more than a bigger chainsaw — they need a real plan: rigging to control where sections fall, the right equipment for the size and location, and a crew that's done this before. We handle large hazardous removals routinely, including trees close to homes, fences, and power lines.

How the removal works, start to finish

  1. Call or request a quote. Tell us what you're seeing — a photo helps, but isn't required — and we'll get a sense of the job before we're on-site.
  2. On-site walkthrough. We look at the tree, the drop zone, access, and anything nearby (structures, fences, power lines, septic lines) that affects how the job is done, then give you a firm price.
  3. Scheduling. Routine removals are typically scheduled within the week; hazardous or storm-related trees are prioritized sooner. See our emergency & storm damage page if the tree is actively unstable.
  4. The removal itself. Depending on size and access, the tree is either felled in one piece in open space, or taken down in sections with rigging when there's anything nearby that can't be risked.
  5. Cleanup and haul-away. All debris is cleared unless you've asked to keep firewood rounds or have brush mulched on-site instead.

Insurance matters more than most homeowners realize

Tree removal, especially near a house, fence, or power line, carries real risk if something goes wrong mid-job — and that risk shouldn't fall on you. Ask to see proof of insurance before any crew, ours or anyone else's, touches your property. It's a completely reasonable request, and a legitimate operator will hand it over without hesitation. An uninsured crew offering a suspiciously low bid is one of the most common ways homeowners end up covering damage costs themselves.

Common situations in East Texas

Longview and the surrounding area sit in the piney woods, which means a few specific issues come up more here than in other parts of Texas. Loblolly pines are common, fast-growing, and prone to failure when stressed by drought or infested by southern pine bark beetle — a pine with reddish, browning needles or resin streaming down the trunk should be looked at quickly, since beetle infestations can spread to nearby trees. Mature post oaks and red oaks are common in older Longview neighborhoods and tend to develop structural issues (deadwood, included bark, root damage from nearby construction or trenching) as they age. And because East Texas gets regular severe thunderstorms, we see a steady stream of storm-stressed trees that looked fine before a big wind event and don't afterward — that's covered in more detail on our emergency & storm damage page.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit to remove a tree in Longview?

Requirements can depend on your specific property, the tree, and the situation. Give us a call and we'll help you figure out what, if anything, applies before any work begins.

How long does a typical removal take?

Most single-tree removals are completed in a few hours to a full day, depending on size and access.

What happens to the wood?

We haul it away as part of standard service; if you'd like to keep firewood-length rounds, just ask — we're happy to leave them.

Do you grind the stump too?

Yes, as an add-on — see our Stump Grinding page.

Is someone going to be on my roof or near my power lines?

Only when necessary, and only with the right rigging and precautions — we'll explain exactly what the job involves before we start.

Do I need to be home?

Not necessarily, but we recommend it for the initial walkthrough so we can point out anything you should know about the tree or the property.

What if it rains on the scheduled day?

We'll call to reschedule — safety comes first, especially with larger removals.

Can you remove just one tree out of a cluster without damaging the others?

Yes — this is routine, and it's exactly the kind of job where rigging matters. We section the tree down carefully rather than dropping it whole, so the trees around it aren't at risk.

Will removing a tree damage my lawn or driveway?

We take reasonable precautions (plywood or mats under heavy equipment paths, careful rigging to control where debris lands), but some ground disturbance from equipment access is normal on a real removal job. We'll walk you through what to expect for your specific property during the on-site assessment.
