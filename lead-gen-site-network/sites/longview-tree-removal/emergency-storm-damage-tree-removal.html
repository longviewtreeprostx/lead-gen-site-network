East Texas storms don't give much warning, and a damaged or fallen tree is one of the most dangerous things that can happen to a property. If a tree has come down or is leaning after high winds, ice, or heavy rain, don't wait — call us. We prioritize storm response and hazardous-lean situations ahead of routine scheduling.

What to do right now

  • Stay away from any tree or limb that's touching a power line — treat every downed line as live and call the utility company, not us, for that specifically
  • Don't go near a leaning or partially-fallen tree — it can shift or drop without warning
  • If it's safe to do so, take photos of the damage for your insurance claim before anything is moved
  • Call us for an assessment — we'll tell you honestly whether it's a true emergency or something that can be scheduled in the next day or two

What counts as a true emergency

Not every storm-damaged tree needs a crew on-site within the hour, and we'll tell you honestly when that's the case instead of upselling urgency you don't need. A true emergency is a tree or large limb actively resting on a structure, vehicle, or driveway blocking access; a tree visibly leaning or partially uprooted with roots pulling out of the ground; or a large hanging limb (sometimes called a "widowmaker") caught in the canopy above a walkway, driveway, or anywhere people pass underneath. If what you're describing is a fallen limb in the yard with nothing at risk, or storm damage that's cosmetic rather than structural, that's a same-week job, not a 2 a.m. dispatch — and we'll say so rather than pad the bill with unnecessary rush pricing.

Common storm-damage scenarios

Tree on a house or roof

The highest-priority call we get. Depending on the size of the tree and how it's resting, removal may need to happen in careful stages to avoid additional structural damage while the tree is being taken off the roof — this is not a job for a chainsaw and good intentions.

Tree on a vehicle or fence

Common after high wind events. We coordinate timing with your insurance adjuster's inspection when needed, but won't leave a hazardous tree in place longer than necessary just to wait on paperwork — ask and we'll walk you through the tradeoffs.

Leaning or partially uprooted tree

One of the most dangerous situations on this list, because the tree can still be standing and look stable while the root system underneath has already failed. Keep people, pets, and vehicles away and call for an assessment rather than waiting to see if it falls the rest of the way on its own.

Response time, honestly

We prioritize emergency and storm calls ahead of routine scheduling, but exact response time depends on how many storm calls are coming in at once during severe weather — the same storm that damaged your tree is usually damaging trees across the whole service area at the same time. We'll give you a realistic estimate when you call, not a vague "we'll be right there" that doesn't hold up. If a hazard is genuinely immediate (a tree actively resting on a structure with someone inside, for example), tell us that clearly when you call so it's triaged correctly.

Insurance claim guidance

Most storm-damage tree removal gets filed as a homeowner's insurance claim. A few things that help your claim move faster: photograph the damage from multiple angles before any cleanup starts, note the date and approximate time of the storm, and keep a copy of our invoice/estimate — insurers typically want an itemized description of the work, not just a total. We're glad to provide documentation your adjuster can use, and we can talk you through what's usually covered (tree removal is commonly covered when the tree damaged a covered structure; removal of a fallen tree that caused no damage sometimes is not — check your specific policy).

After the storm: what to expect

Once you call, we'll ask a few quick questions (what fell, what it's resting on or near, whether anyone's at risk) to gauge priority before we ever discuss price. For genuine emergencies, we move ahead of the routine schedule. For everything else, we'll be straight with you about realistic timing rather than promising something we can't deliver during a busy storm week. Either way, the same full-service approach applies once we're on-site: careful removal, complete cleanup and haul-away, and documentation for your insurance claim if you need it.

Frequently asked questions

Do you offer 24-hour emergency service?

We prioritize genuine emergencies (a tree on a structure, an actively failing lean) ahead of routine scheduling, including outside normal hours when the situation calls for it. Call and describe what you're seeing — we'll tell you honestly how quickly we can respond.

Is emergency removal more expensive than a regular removal?

Genuine after-hours or storm-priority work can carry a premium over routine scheduling, reflecting the urgency and crew availability involved — but we'll always quote you a firm price before starting, not a number that grows once we're on-site.

A power line is involved — do you handle that?

No — any tree or limb touching a power line, or a downed line itself, should be treated as live and reported to the utility company, not a tree service. We won't work near an energized line; once the utility company has cleared it, we can safely handle the tree work.

Should I move debris before you arrive?

Generally no — leave it as-is (photograph it first for insurance purposes) unless it's blocking emergency access to your home or creating an immediate additional hazard. Let us assess the full situation on-site.

Do you work directly with insurance adjusters?

We can provide itemized documentation and photos your adjuster can use, and coordinate timing around an inspection when that's reasonable. The claim itself is between you and your insurer, but we'll make sure you have what you need to support it.

What if the tree is on my neighbor's property but leaning toward mine?

This comes up more than you'd think. Whose responsibility it is can depend on the specifics, but the immediate safety question is separate from that — call for an assessment either way, and we can talk through next steps once we've seen it.
