Why it matters

  • Storm resilience — dead and weak limbs are what typically fail first in high wind
  • Tree health — proper pruning encourages stronger growth and helps trees resist disease
  • Property value and curb appeal — well-maintained trees are a visible sign of a cared-for property
  • Safety and visibility — keeping branches clear of walkways, driveways, and power lines

Timing for East Texas species

Pines generally tolerate trimming year-round, but late winter (before spring growth) is ideal for shaping and deadwood removal. Oaks are the exception — avoid pruning live oaks and red oaks from February through June in this region, when oak wilt transmission risk is highest; late fall through mid-winter is the safer window. We'll advise on timing specific to your trees when we assess your property.

Trimming vs. pruning, explained simply

"Trimming" and "pruning" get used interchangeably, but there's a real difference: trimming is general shaping and controlling size/growth, while pruning is more targeted — selectively removing specific branches for the tree's health, safety, or structure. Most jobs involve some of both, and we'll explain exactly what we're doing and why.

Signs a tree needs trimming now, not eventually

Routine maintenance trimming can usually wait for the right season, but a few signs mean it's worth scheduling sooner: branches visibly rubbing against your roof, siding, or gutters every time the wind picks up; limbs hanging over a driveway, walkway, or anywhere people or vehicles regularly pass underneath; dense, uneven growth on one side of the canopy that shifts the tree's weight distribution; or any branch making contact with a power line, which should be reported and handled with appropriate caution rather than a DIY ladder job.

Types of pruning we do

Crown thinning

Selectively removing branches throughout the canopy to reduce density and let more light and air through — this reduces wind resistance and storm risk without changing the tree's overall shape or size.

Crown raising

Removing lower branches to clear space above walkways, driveways, and structures, or to improve sightlines and visibility around the property.

Deadwooding

Removing dead, dying, or damaged branches before they fail on their own — often the highest-value trim you can do, since dead limbs are what typically come down first in a storm.

What we don't do is "topping" — cutting a tree's main branches back to stubs to reduce height quickly. It's a common request, but it's genuinely bad for the tree: it triggers weak, fast-growing regrowth that's more likely to fail later, and most reputable arborists consider it poor practice rather than a real pruning technique. If height reduction is truly what you need, crown reduction (selectively shortening branches back to a lateral, rather than stubbing them) achieves a similar result without the long-term damage.

Trimming near power lines

Branches growing into or near power lines are a real hazard and not a job for a homeowner with a pole saw. We handle line-adjacent trimming with the appropriate caution and distance, but for anything directly touching an energized line, that's the utility company's job first — we can advise on what you're looking at and coordinate next steps once it's safe to do so.

Young tree training

Structural pruning on a young tree in its first few years is some of the most valuable trimming you can do, even though it doesn't look like much at the time. Correcting competing leaders, weak branch angles, and crossing limbs early shapes a tree that grows with fewer structural problems down the line — the alternative is often a mature tree with an inherent weak point that eventually needs major surgery, or removal, instead of a light annual trim.

Frequently asked questions

How often should trees be trimmed?

Most residential trees benefit from a trim every 2–3 years; faster-growing species or trees near structures may need more frequent attention. We'll give you a straight recommendation for your specific trees.

Will trimming hurt my tree?

Done correctly, no — proper pruning improves a tree's long-term health. Improper pruning (over-trimming, "topping," cutting at the wrong time of year) is what causes damage, which is exactly what correct technique and timing avoid.

Do you clean up after trimming?

Yes — full cleanup and haul-away of trimmed limbs and debris is included.

How much of the tree can safely be removed in one trim?

A common guideline is no more than about a quarter of the live canopy in a single session — taking significantly more than that stresses the tree and can trigger the same kind of weak regrowth as topping. If a tree needs more than that, it's often better done gradually over a couple of visits.

Can you trim a tree without a ladder touching my roof?

Yes — we use climbing gear or a bucket/lift depending on the job, not ladders propped against your house. We'll assess access during the on-site walkthrough.

Is trimming cheaper than removal?

Usually, yes, for a comparable-sized tree — but it depends on how much work the tree actually needs. See our pricing & cost guide for typical ranges.
